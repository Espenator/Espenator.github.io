What to Look for in a Boutique Resort Investment

Most people fall in love with a resort before they ever look at it. The

light at dusk, the pool that seems to pour into the ocean, the sense that

this place could belong to you. That feeling is real, and it matters — but

it is the beginning of the evaluation, not the end of it.

A boutique resort investment asks two questions at once. Will this property

hold its value, and can it hold you? The first is diligence. The second is

taste. Both deserve a disciplined answer.

Start with the property, not the story

Every resort has a story. Some stories are earned: a family that has run

the place for decades, a chef with a reputation that fills the restaurant

on its own, a beach or a view that cannot be replicated. Others are just

copy on a listing site.

The way to tell the difference is to look for what cannot be moved. The

location is fixed. The building is fixed. The land's relationship to the

sea, the jungle, the town — fixed. Everything else can change: the

management, the brand, the interiors, even the name.

Ask what survives a change of ownership. A good boutique resort keeps its

soul through new stewardship. A fragile one loses its only asset — its

personality — the day the founders leave.

Keys, occupancy, and the right size

Boutique is a word people use loosely. In the serious sense, it means a

property small enough that every guest is known, and large enough that the

operation pays for itself. Twenty rooms is the sweet spot in most Latin

American markets; a ten-room property needs exceptional rates or an

exceptional brand to structure the same economics.

What you are really examining is the balance between intimacy and

sustainability. A resort that is too small cannot absorb a bad month. A

resort that is too large stops being boutique and starts being a machine —

and machines compete on price, which is a race you do not want to be in.

Look at occupancy across the full year, not the peak season snapshot. A

property that runs at high occupancy in July and sits half empty in October

is selling you the average of two very different businesses. Ask for the

twelve-month curve.

The operating team is the asset

Resorts do not run themselves. Behind every exceptional guest experience is

a team — and in the best boutique properties, that team is the reason

guests return.

When you evaluate a resort, look past the building to the people. How long

has the general manager been there? Does the staff know the returning

guests by name? Is the service culture something that survives turnover, or

does it depend on one charismatic person?

A property with a stable, trained team is worth materially more than the

same building with a revolving door. Culture is the hardest thing to build

and the easiest thing to destroy. In due diligence, spend as much time on

the people as on the financials.

Revenue quality matters more than revenue volume

Not all resort revenue is equal. Direct bookings — guests who come to the

property's own website — are the highest-quality revenue a resort can earn.

No commission, no intermediary, a guest who chose this place specifically.

Then look at repeat guests. A resort with a meaningful share of returning

guests has something no marketing budget can buy: proof that the

experience works. Loyalty is the single strongest signal of a property's

long-term value.

Finally, examine the mix. Rooms revenue should structure the operation. If the

property depends on one wedding season or one corporate client to stay

afloat, the concentration risk is yours to structure.

The land and the view are the moat

A boutique resort's most defensible asset is rarely the building. It is the

position: the cove, the ridge, the stretch of beachfront that cannot be

manufactured and will never be made again.

Coastlines are fixed. Views are fixed. The supply of genuinely

extraordinary locations is shrinking every year while demand for them grows.

This is the quiet economics underneath every great resort investment: the

land appreciates while the building is maintained.

That said, respect the maintenance. Tropical climates are unforgiving, and

deferred maintenance compounds. A property that looks beautiful in photos

may be carrying years of postponed capital expenditure. Walk the back of

house. Look at the roofs, the water systems, the staff quarters. The truth

of a resort lives where the guests never go.

Legal structure: know exactly what you own

This is the least romantic part of the evaluation and the most important.

In Latin America, property structures vary enormously: full fee ownership,

concession land, long-term leases, fideicomiso trust structures in Mexico,

maritime-zone rules in Costa Rica.

Before anything else, understand exactly what form of ownership you are

buying — and what it allows. Can the land be sold? Leased? Developed

further? What happens at the end of a concession term?

A clean structure with verified title is non-negotiable. If the ownership

question has asterisks, the investment has asterisks, no matter how

beautiful the lobby.

The local market has a say

A resort does not exist in isolation. The destination shapes everything:

air access, tourism growth, infrastructure, safety, the direction of the

place over the next decade.

Look for destinations with improving access — new flight routes, better

roads, investment in the tourism corridor. A resort in a market that is

gaining momentum is carried by the tide. A resort in a market that is

fading has to swim against it.

At the same time, be wary of destinations that have already peaked as

speculative plays. The time to own the beautiful place is before everyone

knows it is beautiful, and after the infrastructure exists to reach it.

That window is narrow, and finding it is the whole game.

Tokenized ownership changes the question

For decades, investing in a boutique resort meant one thing: buying the

whole property, or a conventional share of it, with all the friction that

entails. The minimums were high, the paperwork was heavy, and the exit was

slow.
