Sell Down a Commercial Mortgage, Carefully

To sell down a commercial mortgage is to sell a part of a loan you hold, while the loan itself stays exactly what it was. A bank, a fund, or a private lender holds a mortgage against a commercial property. Instead of holding the whole position alone, the holder transfers a defined share to another holder. The borrower keeps paying on the same loan. The property keeps standing behind it. What changes is who holds the claim, and in what proportion.

This is an old practice with a plain purpose. A loan that was right for one balance sheet can become too large a share of it, too concentrated in one property or one town. Selling down lets the original holder keep the relationship and a considered share, while another holder, who has studied the same documents, takes the rest. Done well, it is quiet, documented, and unhurried. Done carelessly, it creates two holders who understood two different loans.

This article explains what a sell-down is, how its two common forms differ, where each holder stands, and how Embodier approaches this work. It is education, not advice. Not a coin. Not theater. Real property, real documents, and a collection built slowly, because a collection is defined by what it keeps.

What selling down means

Begin with the loan as it stands today. A commercial mortgage is a written promise by a borrower to repay money lent against a commercial property, secured by that property under the law of the place where it stands. The holder holds a contract and a recorded security, with a borrower, an operator behind the property, and a history of payments that can be read.

A sell-down divides that holding. The original holder keeps a portion and transfers a portion. The borrower's promise is not rewritten by the division, and the security recorded against the property does not move. The division lives in new documents between the holders, stating who holds which share, who speaks to the borrower, who receives and divides payments as they arrive, and what happens if the borrower falls behind.

The two forms a sell-down usually takes

The first and more common form is a participation. The original holder remains the lender of record. The incoming holder takes a participation share: an interest in the loan held through the original holder, governed by a participation agreement. The borrower continues to deal with the one lender it knows. Behind that single relationship, the holders share the position in the proportions their agreement states, and the agreement settles how payments are passed on, how decisions are made, which decisions need every holder's consent, and how a problem with the borrower is handled together.

The second form is an assignment. Here a defined part, or the whole, of the loan is transferred directly, and the incoming holder becomes a lender of record in its own name for the share it takes. The borrower is told, in writing, who now holds what. Assignment gives the incoming holder a more direct standing. Participation keeps the original structure intact and the borrower's world unchanged. Neither form is better in the abstract. The right form depends on the loan, the place, and what each holder needs its standing to be.

A careful conversation names the form early, because standing follows form, and a misunderstanding about it is preventable on paper, before anything moves.

Why a holder sells down a position it likes

The reasons are usually about shape, not about doubt. A holder may have lent well and still hold too much of one property for the size of its own book. A holder may want room to lend to a new borrower without calling the existing loan or pressing the borrower in any way. A holder may be rebalancing toward another region or another kind of property, slowly, in the way a collection is adjusted over years.

There is also the relationship reason, respected in this field. The original lender often knows the borrower, the property, and the town. Selling down a share lets that lender stay in place and keep its word to the borrower, while the commitment is shared with a holder who was chosen, not found in a hurry.

What a sell-down is not is an exit from a problem, dressed as an opportunity. A holder who knows the borrower is faltering, and sells a share without putting that knowledge fully on the table, has damaged more than one loan. A seller's standing in a small, long-memoried field rests on the completeness of what it shared.

Where each holder stands

Standing is the centre of this subject. In a commercial mortgage, the lender's claim stands ahead of the owner's equity in the property. That senior standing is given by the loan documents and the recorded security, and it is the borrower's promise, backed by the building, that every holder shares.

A sell-down divides that claim among holders without moving it up or down. Each holder owns the share the division documents give it, with the standing the original loan carries, shared in proportion, unless the documents state a different arrangement in clear language. This is why the inter-holder documents matter as much as the mortgage itself. They answer, before anyone needs to ask: who decides if the borrower asks for more time, who decides if the loan must be enforced, how collections are shared, and how information reaches every holder at the same time, in the same form.

In several jurisdictions, a neutral party holds the recorded security for the benefit of all holders. Used well, that keeps a divided position orderly: one record, one security, several holders whose shares are defined in their own documents. The structure serves the standing. It never replaces it.

Underwritten again, to a bank standard

A sell-down is underwritten twice: once when the loan was made, and again when a share of it moves. The second reading is not a courtesy copy of the first. The incoming holder, and any serious structuring desk, reads the position as it stands now.

That reading begins with the property's real records. Operating statements are reconciled openly against bank statements and tax filings, across a full span of recent history, so the numbers describe money that actually moved. A figure that cannot be reconciled is treated as a figure that does not yet exist. The payment history of the loan is read the same way, from records rather than from memory.

Then the building and the people. An independent appraisal and a physical review describe the property as it is today, not as it was when the loan was made. The borrower and the operator are studied again. Coverage is studied in an ordinary year and in a slower one, and the end of the loan too: how the borrower is expected to repay or refinance at term, under conditions less friendly than today's.

Only when those questions have written answers are the size and terms of a share discussed, and those terms live where they belong: in the offering and transaction documents, read in full by anyone considering a position. This article describes structure and standing, not the terms of a specific loan.

What stays with the original holder

Unless the documents say otherwise, the original holder keeps the working life of the loan. It services the position: collecting payments, watching the property's reports, speaking with the borrower, and passing each holder its share and its information. That continuity is often the point. The borrower sees the lender it knows, and the incoming holder receives a serviced position.

Continuity carries duties, and the documents should name them: how often holders receive statements, how quickly a late payment is reported, and which borrower requests must be brought to every holder first.

The risks, stated plainly

A divided loan carries the risks of the loan itself, and a few of its own. The borrower can fall behind, and enforcing a security takes time, cost, and local legal process, during which the property must still be cared for. A season can disappoint. An operator can falter. Currency and local law can change around a property.

To those, the structure adds its own list. A participation holder depends on the original holder's administration and standing, which is why the participation agreement and the seller's own strength are read as part of the position. Holders can disagree about a borrower in difficulty, which is why decision rules are written before they are needed. And a share of a single loan is a position to hold with a long horizon, within the transfer rules of its documents, not something to move on a restless afternoon.

None of this is a reason for fear. All of it is a reason to read and to hold only what one understands.
